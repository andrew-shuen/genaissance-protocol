Genetics 101

A plain-language primer on genes and how conditions are inherited.

This primer explains the words you will see in the protocols. You do not need any science background. If a term in your report is not covered here, ask your genetics team.

What is a gene?

Your body is built and run from a set of instructions called DNA. DNA is divided into sections, and each section that carries the instructions for one job is called a gene. A gene might tell the body how to repair damaged cells, how to build a protein, or how to control growth.

You have roughly 20,000 genes. You inherit two copies of almost every gene: one from your biological mother and one from your biological father.

Think of DNA as a very long cookbook, and each gene as a single recipe in it.

What is a variant (or mutation)?

A variant is a change in the spelling of a gene. Some variants make no difference. Some change how the gene works. When a variant stops a gene from doing its job properly, it can raise the risk of a health condition.

Laboratories do not simply call a variant "good" or "bad." Each is placed on a five-step scale based on how strong the evidence is. The two ends, benign and pathogenic, are confident calls. The steps beside them, likely benign and likely pathogenic, lean one way but with less certainty. In the middle sits the VUS, a variant of uncertain significance, where there is not yet enough evidence to say which way it falls. A VUS is not a diagnosis, and it is not used to guide treatment; many are eventually reclassified toward benign as more data accumulate.

A typo in a recipe. Most typos you would never notice; a few change the dish, and sometimes you cannot yet tell which.

How conditions are inherited

Because you carry two copies of each gene, what matters is whether a condition needs one altered copy or both. The patterns below describe that.

Autosomal dominant (AD)

One altered copy of the gene is enough to raise risk, even though the other copy is normal. A parent who carries it has, on average, a 50% chance of passing it to each child. Most hereditary cancer genes, including BRCA1, BRCA2, and TP53, follow this pattern.

De novo (a new variant)

Sometimes a dominant variant is not inherited from either parent but appears for the first time in the child, arising in the egg, the sperm, or early development. This is called a de novo variant. Neither parent carries it, yet the child is affected. The child can still pass it on to their own children in the usual autosomal dominant way, with a 50% chance each. A de novo finding often means the chance of the same condition in the parents' future children is low, though not zero.

Autosomal recessive (AR)

Both copies of the gene must be altered for the condition to appear. A person with only one altered copy is a "carrier," usually healthy, but able to pass that copy on. A condition appears when a child inherits an altered copy from each parent. MUTYH-associated polyposis follows this pattern.

X-linked

The altered gene sits on the X chromosome, one of the two chromosomes that determine sex. Males have one X and one Y; females have two X chromosomes. Because of this, an X-linked condition affects males and females differently, and there are two patterns depending on whether one altered copy is enough.

X-linked recessive

A male is affected if his single X carries the variant, because he has no second X to compensate. A female usually needs both of her X copies altered to be affected, so she is typically an unaffected carrier with just one. Most carrier mothers are healthy but can pass the variant on.

X-linked dominant

One altered X copy is enough to cause the condition. An affected mother carries the variant on one of her two X chromosomes, so each child, son or daughter, has about a 50% chance of inheriting it and being affected. Affected males often have more pronounced features than affected females, because they have no second X to offset the change.

Polygenic

Risk comes not from one altered gene but from the combined effect of many common genetic differences, each adding a small amount. This is summarised as a polygenic risk score (PRS). Unlike the patterns above, polygenic risk is not passed down in a simple yes or no way; it reflects the overall mix of variants a person happens to inherit.

Mitochondrial inheritance

A small number of genes sit outside the cell nucleus, inside the mitochondria, the structures that power our cells. These genes are passed down almost entirely through the egg, so a condition caused by a mitochondrial gene comes from the mother and can be passed by a mother to all of her children, sons and daughters alike. Fathers do not pass these genes on. The amount of altered mitochondrial DNA can vary from person to person, which is one reason the same variant can cause different degrees of illness within a family.

Epigenetic imprinting

For most genes, the copy from each parent works the same way. For a few genes, only one parent's copy is normally switched on, while the other is kept quiet, a process called imprinting. For these genes the parent the copy came from matters. If the working copy is missing or altered, a condition can appear even though the other copy looks normal, because that other copy was never meant to be active. This is why a handful of conditions depend not just on which variant is inherited, but on whether it came from the mother or the father.

A few more terms you may see

Carrier

Someone who has one altered copy of a recessive gene. Usually healthy, but can pass the copy to children.

Penetrance

The chance that someone carrying a harmful variant actually develops the related condition. High penetrance means most carriers are affected; lower penetrance means many are not. This is why a variant raises risk rather than guaranteeing an outcome.

Cascade testing

What it means when a genetic finding is shared with your relatives.

When a pathogenic variant is found in one person, that same variant may run in the family. Cascade testing is the step-by-step process of offering testing to relatives, starting with those most closely related.

How it works

The first person found to carry a variant is called the proband. Because most hereditary conditions are autosomal dominant, each first-degree relative (parents, siblings, children) has a 50% chance of carrying the same variant. Testing moves outward in steps: once a relative tests positive, their own first-degree relatives are then offered testing, and so on through the family.

Why it matters

Relatives who carry the variant can begin surveillance or prevention before any cancer or condition develops. Relatives who test negative for the known family variant can usually step back to average-risk care, which spares them unnecessary screening. Testing a relative is simpler and less expensive once the exact variant is known, because the laboratory looks only for that single change.

Sharing with family

Genetic results belong to you, and the clinic cannot contact your relatives directly. Many families use a brief family letter, which your genetics team can provide, that explains the finding and how relatives can arrange their own testing. You decide who to share it with and when.

Your genetics team can help you identify which relatives to inform and provide a family letter to make the conversation easier.

Genetic privacy & GINA

How genetic test results are protected from discrimination.

A common worry before genetic testing is whether a result could be used against you. Several laws protect genetic information, though the specifics differ between the United States and Canada.

GINA (United States)

The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 is a US federal law. It prevents health insurers from using genetic information to deny coverage or set premiums, and prevents employers from using it in hiring, firing, or promotion decisions. GINA has two important limits: it does not apply to life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term care insurance, and it does not apply to members of the military or some federal programs.

In Canada

The Genetic Non-Discrimination Act, a federal law upheld by the Supreme Court of Canada in 2020, makes it illegal to require a genetic test, or to require disclosure of an existing result, as a condition of providing goods, services, or a contract such as insurance. It also prohibits genetic discrimination in employment. Provincial human rights protections and health privacy laws, including Quebec's framework, add further safeguards.

What this means for you

For most people pursuing testing for medical care, these protections cover the situations of greatest concern, particularly health insurance and employment. Because life and disability insurance are treated differently across jurisdictions, it is reasonable to ask your genetics team how the rules apply to your situation before testing if this is a concern for you.

This is general information, not legal advice. Laws change and vary by jurisdiction; confirm current protections relevant to your situation.

Cancer genetics 101

What it means for a cancer risk to run in a family.

Most cancers are not inherited. They come from damage that builds up in a single cell over a lifetime, by chance and from exposures such as tobacco, sun, or simply ageing. A smaller share, roughly 5 to 10 percent, begins with an inherited variant that raises risk from birth. This section explains that smaller, important group.

Sporadic, familial, and hereditary

Sporadic cancer happens by chance with no single inherited cause, and is the most common kind. Familial cancer is when a type of cancer appears in a family somewhat more than expected, likely from a mix of shared genes and environment, but without one clear high-risk variant. Hereditary cancer is when a single inherited variant in a known gene strongly raises risk, often follows an autosomal dominant pattern, and is what genetic testing looks for.

Somatic versus germline changes

A genetic change can arise in two very different ways. A germline change is present in the egg or sperm, so it is carried in every cell of the body from birth and can be passed to children. A somatic change happens later, in a single body cell during life, often from sun, tobacco, or ordinary copying errors as cells divide. A somatic change is found only in that cell and the tissue it grows into, not throughout the body, and it is not passed to children. Most cancers are driven by somatic changes that accumulate in one tissue over time.

Why one inherited variant raises risk: the two-hit idea

Many cancer-protective genes work in pairs, one copy from each parent. A cell is usually safe as long as one working copy remains. Someone born with one altered copy already has, in effect, one strike against them in every cell. It then takes only one further change in the remaining copy, acquired over life, for that protection to be lost in a cell. Because the first change is present from birth, people with a hereditary variant tend to develop cancer earlier and sometimes in more than one place.

Two kinds of cancer gene: accelerators and brakes

The genes involved in cancer fall into two broad groups, and a car is a useful picture. Tumour suppressor genes are the brakes: they hold cell growth in check. Because a cell has two copies, both must be lost before the brakes fail, which is the two-hit idea above. Most hereditary cancer genes, such as BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, and the Lynch genes, are tumour suppressors. Oncogenes are the accelerator: when a single copy is switched into overdrive by an activating change, it pushes the cell to divide. Only one copy needs to change for an oncogene to act, so it behaves dominantly at the cell level. In inherited cancer, tumour suppressors are by far the more common players.

Clues that risk may be inherited

Several patterns in a personal or family history make an inherited cause more likely: cancer at a younger age than usual; the same or related cancers in several close relatives; more than one separate cancer in the same person; certain specific cancers, such as ovarian or male breast cancer; and a known variant already identified in the family. Any one of these can be a reason to consider genetic counselling. They raise the question; they do not by themselves mean a variant is present.

What a result can offer

Finding a hereditary cause is not only information; it opens options. It can mean earlier or more frequent screening, ways to lower risk, and choices that fit your situation. It also lets relatives be tested for the same variant, so those who carry it can act early and those who do not can step back to average-risk care. The gene-specific protocols in this library set out what surveillance and prevention look like for each condition.

A third kind of result: the VUS

Sometimes testing finds a change whose effect is not yet known. This is called a variant of uncertain significance, or VUS. It is not a yes and not a no; there is simply not enough evidence yet to say whether it raises cancer risk. In hereditary cancer this distinction matters a great deal, because a VUS is not used to guide care. Surveillance, risk-reducing surgery, and treatment decisions are based on your personal and family history, not on a VUS. Acting on an uncertain result, for example removing an organ, could cause harm for no proven benefit.

Most variants of uncertain significance are eventually reclassified, and the majority are downgraded toward benign as laboratories gather more data. Your genetics team keeps track of a VUS and will let you know if its classification changes. A VUS is also not used to test relatives, because it cannot yet tell carriers apart from non-carriers in a meaningful way.

Triplet repeat expansions

When a short stretch of repeated DNA grows too long.

Some genetic conditions are not caused by a spelling change in a gene, but by a short sequence of DNA letters that is repeated too many times. Everyone has these repeats; the question is how many. Beyond a certain number, the gene stops working normally and a condition can appear.

A repeated unit, copied over and over

The repeated unit is usually three letters long, which is why these are called triplet repeats. A common example is CAG, repeated in a row: CAG-CAG-CAG and so on. A typical gene has a modest number of these. In a repeat expansion, the same unit is present many more times than usual.

A threshold for disease

For each repeat condition, the number of copies falls into ranges. A normal range causes no problem. An intermediate range is longer than usual but not yet causing disease, though it can be unstable when passed on. A full expansion is above the disease threshold. The exact numbers differ from one condition to another.

Anticipation: growing across generations

When a repeat is in the intermediate or expanded range, it can grow longer as it is passed from parent to child. Because a longer repeat often means earlier or more pronounced disease, a condition can appear at a younger age in each successive generation. This pattern is called anticipation, and it is a hallmark of repeat-expansion disorders.

Why this matters for testing

Because the repeat number can change between generations, a parent in the intermediate range may have a child in the full range. Specialised testing counts the number of repeats rather than reading the spelling of a gene. If a repeat condition runs in your family, your genetics team can explain the specific ranges, what the result means, and the implications for relatives.

This is general education and does not replace a consultation. Repeat ranges and their interpretation are condition-specific and best reviewed with your genetics team.

What to expect from testing

The steps from your first visit to your results.

Genetic testing is a process with several steps, not a single appointment. Knowing the path ahead can make it feel more manageable. Here is what it usually looks like in this clinic.

Cost and your choice

When testing is done through this clinic, it is covered by RAMQ; there is no charge to you. For people who do not qualify for publicly funded testing, private options are available, and we can explain those if they apply to you. Testing is always your choice. You can take time to decide, ask questions at any point, and change your mind, even after signing the consent form. Choosing not to test will not affect the rest of your care.

Before the test

Pre-test counselling happens once your family tree and questionnaires are complete, so the visit can focus on your situation. We talk through what the test can and cannot tell you, the possible results, and what each would mean, so you can decide whether to proceed. If testing is appropriate, you sign the consent form electronically through e-JotForm.

Giving a sample

Most testing uses a small blood sample. Once your consent is signed, our administrative staff will call you to book an appointment to come to the Jewish General Hospital for your blood draw, at a time that is convenient for you. When the test is run at a laboratory in another province, a cheek-swab (buccal) kit is mailed to you instead, which you can do at home and send back. Both give the laboratory the DNA it needs.

On the day of your blood draw

A few practical notes for your visit to the Jewish General Hospital: bring your health card (RAMQ) and the appointment details our staff give you over the phone. You do not need to fast; this is a DNA test, so eating and drinking normally beforehand is fine. The draw itself is a single small sample and the appointment is usually brief. If you take blood thinners or have had trouble with blood draws in the past, let the staff know when they call so they can plan accordingly.

Results and follow-up

Results usually take about one to two months. Dr. Shuen reviews the report once it arrives, and a follow-up visit is arranged to go through it with you, most often over Microsoft Teams or by phone. You do not need to call for the results themselves; you will be contacted to arrange the follow-up. If you have any questions in the meantime, or if something changes, such as new symptoms or a new diagnosis in the family, please call our administrative staff and we will be glad to help.

What a result can mean for your family

If a variant is found, your relatives may share the same chance of carrying it, and testing aimed at that specific variant can tell them whether they do. This is called cascade testing, and it is often simpler and faster than the first test in the family. We will talk through who might benefit and how to share the news at your follow-up.

Possible results and what they mean

A positive, a negative, and the in-between.

A genetic test does not simply come back "good" or "bad." There are a few possible outcomes, and what a result means depends on your personal and family history as well as the variant itself. Understanding the possibilities in advance makes the result easier to take in.

A positive result

A positive result means a pathogenic (harmful) variant was found. It often explains the cancer or condition in the family, and it opens clear next steps: a tailored surveillance plan, prevention options, and the ability to test relatives so they can learn their own risk. A positive result is information you can act on.

A negative result is not always the same thing

There are two very different kinds of negative. A true negative happens when a specific harmful variant is already known in your family and you did not inherit it; here your risk usually steps back toward the general population. An uninformative negative happens when no variant is found but your personal or family history still points to raised risk. In that case the test simply did not find an explanation, and your care continues to be guided by your history rather than by the negative result. This is one of the most commonly misunderstood outcomes: a normal test does not erase a strong family history.

An uncertain result (VUS)

Sometimes a change is found whose effect is not yet known, called a variant of uncertain significance. It is not used to guide your care, and it is not used to test relatives. Most are eventually reclassified, usually toward benign, as laboratories gather more data, and your genetics team keeps track of it. The cancer genetics primer covers the VUS in more detail.

What a result means for you depends on your full history and is best interpreted with your genetics team.

Risk is not certainty

Penetrance, and absolute versus relative risk.

One of the hardest parts of a genetic result to take in is what the numbers actually mean. Carrying a high-risk variant raises the chance of a condition; it rarely makes it certain. A few simple ideas make risk figures easier to read.

Penetrance: how often a variant leads to disease

Penetrance is the chance that someone carrying a harmful variant actually develops the related condition over their lifetime. High penetrance means most carriers are affected; lower penetrance means many are not. This is why a variant raises risk rather than guaranteeing an outcome, and why two people with the same variant can have very different futures.

Absolute versus relative risk

Risk can be described in two ways, and they can sound very different. Relative risk compares carriers to non-carriers, so "three times higher" sounds alarming but does not tell you the actual chance. Absolute risk is your real chance over a given time, for example a certain percent by age 70. A large relative increase on a small baseline can still be a modest absolute risk. When you hear a number, the useful question is whether it is relative or absolute.

Risk figures also change with age, family history, and steps you can take, such as surveillance and prevention. Your genetics team can give you the absolute, age-specific numbers for your particular variant and explain what they mean for your choices.
